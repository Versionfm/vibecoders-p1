## GAIF-1 AI and philosophy

### GAIF-1.1 Can machines think?
The course opens with the old question of whether machines can think, which is as much philosophical as technical. Answering it depends on what we mean by intelligence, mind, cognition, thinking and consciousness (GAIF-1.4), and on whether behaving intelligently is the same as understanding.

### GAIF-1.2 Alan Turing and the Turing Test
Alan Turing was an English mathematician, computer scientist, logician, cryptanalyst, philosopher and theoretical biologist. He formalised the concepts of algorithm and computation with the Turing machine, a model of a general-purpose computer, and is widely considered the father of theoretical computer science and artificial intelligence. The Turing Test (the "imitation game") proposes that a machine should be considered intelligent if a human interrogator, exchanging written messages with a hidden human and a hidden machine, cannot reliably tell which is which. The lecture presents the test as historically important but now obsolete as a measure of intelligence: modern chatbots can imitate human conversation without that settling whether they think.

### GAIF-1.3 ELIZA, Sophia and the Chinese Room
**ELIZA** was an early natural language processing program built at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory between 1964 and 1966 by Joseph Weizenbaum. He intended it to show how superficial communication between people and machines is, and was surprised by how many people — including his own secretary — attributed human-like feelings to it. **Sophia** is a humanoid robot, shown in the lecture through a video, as a modern example of a machine that people readily treat as human-like. **The Chinese Room** is a thought experiment by philosopher John Searle ("Minds, Brains and Programs", 1980): a person who does not understand Chinese sits in a room with a rule book and, by following the rules, returns correct Chinese answers to Chinese questions passed in. From outside the room appears to understand Chinese, but nobody inside does. Searle's point is that manipulating symbols according to rules (syntax) is not the same as understanding meaning (semantics), so a program that behaves intelligently does not thereby have a mind — an argument against "strong AI", the idea that a suitably programmed computer literally has a mind. The lecture links the notion of strong AI to Ray Kurzweil's book *The Singularity Is Near* (see GAIF-1.5).

### GAIF-1.4 Key concepts: intelligence, mind, cognition, thinking, consciousness
- **Intelligence** — the human faculty of thinking and understanding. It has many definitions: the capacity for abstraction, logic, understanding, self-awareness, learning, emotional knowledge, reasoning, planning, creativity, critical thinking and problem solving; or the ability to perceive or infer information and retain it as knowledge applied to adaptive behaviour in an environment.
- **Mind** — the way a person thinks, reasons, perceives, wills, has ideas and feels; a person's set of intellectual and mental faculties. In this view the mind is entirely caused by the workings of the brain. **Functionalists** say the mind is what the brain *does* (a procedural view), so the same thought processes could run in a very different physical medium — an alien with different biology could still think. **Identity theorists** say the mind *is* the brain and nothing more. Gilbert Ryle called the idea of a mind separate from the brain the "ghost in the machine" and rejected it as a false official doctrine, while **Cartesians (dualists)** hold that the mind (the soul) is separate from the body.
- **Cognition** — the mental action or process of acquiring knowledge and understanding through thought, experience and the senses. It covers perception, attention, thinking, imagination, intelligence, knowledge formation, memory and working memory, judgement and evaluation, reasoning and calculation, problem solving and decision making, comprehension and language production. Cognitive processes use existing knowledge and discover new knowledge.
- **Thinking** — conscious cognitive processes that can happen independently of sensory stimulation.
- **Consciousness** — the awareness of inner and outer existence; the state of being aware of and responsive to one's surroundings. Its nature is intensely debated among philosophers, theologians, linguists and scientists: for some it is synonymous with the mind, for others an aspect of it; it once meant one's "inner life" of introspection and private thought, and today can include any kind of knowledge, experience, feeling or perception, awareness, or awareness of awareness.

### GAIF-1.5 Views on machine intelligence: Kurzweil, McGinn and Damasio
**Ray Kurzweil**, an American inventor and futurist, argued in *The Age of Spiritual Machines* (1999) that we will one day create machines more intelligent than humans, predicting human-level machine intelligence within about twenty years, nanotechnology that would enhance our bodies and cure cancer, direct neural interfaces between humans and computers, humans living forever because humanity and its machines merge, and intelligence eventually spreading beyond Earth. Raw computing power is only one component of that vision. Philosopher **Colin McGinn** (2013) argued that Kurzweil's theory is wrong, calling it "homunculism": wrongly attributing human-like qualities to parts of a human, which creates the illusion that we understand how firing synapses become, say, an appreciation of music. Neuroscientist **António Damásio** argues that feelings and emotions are what make up human intelligence, consciousness and the capacity for cultural creation; a map of the computational mind is not the same as being human, so human intelligence cannot simply be transferred to machines.

### GAIF-1.6 Superintelligence
Philosopher Nick Bostrom (University of Oxford) defines superintelligence as any intellect that greatly exceeds human cognitive performance in virtually all domains of interest. Possible pathways discussed are biological cognition (enhancing human brains), whole brain emulation (copying a brain in a computer) and artificial intelligence. When — or whether — this is possible is an open question.

### GAIF-1.7 Does it matter? Ethics and the cost of convenience
Most people care less about whether AI has a mind or a conscience than about having their lives made easier with minimal effort, and AI can do a lot to help. But there is a price. A study by Microsoft with Carnegie Mellon University researchers surveyed 319 knowledge workers about how they used AI; it was reported in the press under the headline that Microsoft "admits AI is making us dumb", even as the company planned to invest $80 billion in AI. Initiatives such as Portuguese symposia on philosophy and artificial intelligence and MIT work bridging philosophy and AI on computing ethics show that these philosophical questions feed directly into the ethics of AI.

## GAIF-2 Foundations of neural networks

### GAIF-2.1 The biological neuron: structure
The brain of an average adult man weighs about 1,375 grams and is not fully formed until about age 25; development runs from the back to the front, so the frontal lobes, which control planning and reasoning, are the last to mature. The generic neuron studied in the lecture is modelled on the spinal cord motor neurons, the most studied in mammals. The brain's elementary computing units are **neurons** (nerve cells): between about 86 billion and 100 billion of them, each connected to hundreds or thousands of others, for an estimated 10¹³ to 10¹⁵ connections in total. That is still only an infinitesimal fraction of the connections that would exist if every neuron were connected to every other. Neurons both cooperate and compete. The brain runs on roughly 23 watts — about enough to power a light bulb. A neuron receives signals through its **dendrites**, integrates them in the cell body (**soma**, containing the nucleus), and sends its output along the **axon**; at the ends of the axon branches are **synapses**, which connect to the dendrites of other neurons. The lecture also compares scale with large AI models, noting that model sizes for recent GPT versions are not officially disclosed and only estimated.

### GAIF-2.2 The biological neuron: how signals work
Inside and outside the neuron there are opposite concentrations of ions (sodium, potassium, chloride). Receptor molecules sit in the dendritic wall and transmitter molecules in the synapses. At rest the axon sits at a **resting potential** of about −70 mV. When the incoming stimulation crosses a threshold, the neuron fires an **action potential**: a voltage spike that travels along the axon and keeps its amplitude even when the axon splits. A stronger stimulus does not produce a bigger spike; it produces a faster regular series of spikes — the intensity is coded in the firing frequency. When an action potential reaches a synapse, **neurotransmitters** are released, bind to receptors and let ions into the next neuron's dendrites, creating **postsynaptic potentials** that are either **excitatory** (opening sodium/potassium channels, pushing toward firing) or **inhibitory** (activating chloride/potassium channels, holding the membrane below the firing level). A neuron can receive around 10,000 such inputs; if excitation outweighs inhibition and the threshold is reached, it fires and the cycle repeats. After firing there is an absolute refractory period of 1–2 ms in which it cannot fire again, capping the maximum firing rate at about 500–1,000 Hz (some neurons have been measured firing at around 50 Hz). The efficiency of synapses varies, and these efficiencies are the key to understanding neural computation — the idea later borrowed by artificial "weights".

### GAIF-2.3 The biological neuron: energy and neurogenesis
The nervous system is only about 1–2% of body weight but consumes about 20% of the body's energy, largely because each neuron constantly runs a sodium pump (expelling sodium and concentrating potassium). Neurons compete to make connections, and those whose connections are wrong die — even before birth up to half of the nerve cells may die — so nature keeps only the neurons that are needed. Neuroscientists long believed we are born with all the neurons we will ever have; later work (Elizabeth Gould in monkeys; Fred Gage and Peter Eriksson in adult humans) found evidence of new neurons forming in adulthood (**neurogenesis**), though its role in learning and memory is still being studied. Because a neuron's action potential, once triggered, does not change shape as the stimulus grows, it behaves almost like the true/false of Boolean logic — the observation behind the first artificial neuron.

### GAIF-2.4 From biology to artificial neural networks
Modelling everything known about real neurons would make neurocomputation impossible with today's technology, so artificial neural networks (ANNs) deliberately ignore most details. They are generalisations of mathematical models of cognition or neurobiology built on four assumptions: information is processed in simple units called neurons; signals pass between neurons through connections; each connection has a **weight** that multiplies the signal it carries; and each neuron applies an **activation function**, usually non-linear, to the weighted sum of its inputs to produce its output.

### GAIF-2.5 The McCulloch-Pitts neuron (1943)
Proposed by Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, it is perhaps the first artificial neuron and the first working archetype of the nervous system built from abstract neurons. Its features: binary activation (it either does not fire, 0, or fires, 1); neurons are linked by weighted connections; a connection is excitatory if its weight is positive and inhibitory if negative, and all excitatory weights into a neuron are equal; each neuron has a **threshold**, and it fires if its net input exceeds it. It is a two-state machine with synapses of equal efficiency and linear interaction, in which inhibition is absolute: if any inhibitory synapse is active, the neuron stays inactive; otherwise it compares the sum of its stimuli with the threshold and fires if the sum is high enough. With suitable weights and thresholds it implements logic gates: **AND** (weights 1 and 1, threshold 2 — fires only when both inputs are on) and **OR** (weights 2 and 2, threshold 2 — fires when either input is on). **XOR** cannot be done by a single neuron; it needs a small two-layer network that combines "A and not B" with "not A and B" using inhibitory (negative) weights. This all-or-nothing neuron was a very reasonable approximation for 1943, but we now know real neurons are not mere two-state machines and do more than formal logic.

### GAIF-2.6 A brief history of neural networks
- **1940s — the beginning:** McCulloch and Pitts's neuron (1943); von Neumann's model of the digital computer (1946); Hebb's learning rule (1949).
- **1950s–60s — a successful first age:** Rosenblatt's single-layer perceptron (1958); Widrow and Hoff's Adaline with least-squares learning (1960); Minsky and Papert's book *Perceptrons* (1969) exposing their limitations.
- **1970s — a quieter period** (an "AI winter"): Kohonen and Anderson's linear associative networks (1972); Grossberg's Adaptive Resonance Theory (1976).
- **1980s — renewed enthusiasm:** Kohonen's self-organising maps and Hopfield's recurrent network (1982); the multilayer perceptron and error backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams, McClelland, 1986); Boltzmann machines.
- **1990s:** support vector machines (Vapnik and others) for regression and pattern recognition.
- **2000s — widespread application** in economics and finance, signal processing, computer vision, robotics, expert systems, statistics and data mining; the term "deep learning" was introduced to neural networks by Igor Aizenberg and colleagues in 2000.
John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton received the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for their work on neural networks.

### GAIF-2.7 How neural networks learn
Learning can be **unsupervised** (the network looks for patterns in the input signals on its own, according to its structure) or **supervised** (a "teacher" presents cases with their inputs and the correct outputs, and the weights change as cases are presented). The rules that decide how weights change are called **learning laws**. A system has learned an association f → g when, given input f, it produces g.

### GAIF-2.8 Hebb's law (1949)
Donald Hebb's rule says a system learns by strengthening connections between units that are active together. In matrix form, to associate an input vector f with an output vector g, the learning matrix is A = η·g·fᵀ (η a learning constant), so that presenting f gives A·f ≈ g. Several associations can be stored in one network by computing a matrix for each pair and adding them (A = A₁ + A₂ + …); presenting a stored input recalls its output, while an input that was never stored produces a weak or empty response. Worked examples in the lecture use bipolar notation (−1 and +1 instead of 0 and 1) and a sign activation (output +1 if the net input is positive, otherwise −1): Hebb's rule learns the AND function, and it can memorise two 4-point patterns (a "square" and a "triangle") and recognise each when presented. As an algorithm: start with all weights at 0; for each training pair (input s, target t), set the inputs xᵢ = sᵢ and the output y = t, then update each weight wᵢ(new) = wᵢ(old) + xᵢ·y and the bias b(new) = b(old) + y. The limitation: with many stored associations, the recalled output stops being exact.

### GAIF-2.9 The perceptron (1958)
Frank Rosenblatt, a psychologist, created the perceptron, the first neural network to become fashionable in science; it seemed to be a learning machine, and its creators claimed it could do almost anything, starting a kind of "magic" around neural networks that persists today. It was also a physical machine: the Mark I Perceptron had randomly wired connections (to show it could learn the right pattern without precise wiring), and its weights were potentiometers adjusted by electric motors. The original perceptron had three layers of units: sensor, association and response. A single perceptron neuron computes its net input y_in = b + Σ xᵢwᵢ and outputs +1 if y_in is above a threshold θ, 0 if it is between −θ and θ, and −1 if it is below −θ. **Training:** initialise the weights and bias (usually 0) and a learning rate α between 0 and 1 (often 1); for each training pair compute the output; if it differs from the target t, update wᵢ(new) = wᵢ(old) + α·t·xᵢ and b(new) = b(old) + α·t, otherwise leave them unchanged; repeat until a stopping condition. **Using it** ("asking"): set the inputs, compute y_in and apply the same output rule. In 1969 Minsky and Papert's book *Perceptrons* showed that perceptrons also have important theoretical limitations (see GAIF-2.11); a single neuron, for example, cannot compute XOR (GAIF-2.5).

### GAIF-2.10 Adaline and the delta rule (1960)
Adaline (Adaptive Linear Neuron), by Bernard Widrow and Marcian (Ted) Hoff at Stanford, is a single-layer network for binary classification and regression. Like the perceptron it computes a weighted sum, but it uses a linear activation with a continuous output and is trained to **minimise error**, not just to get the classification right. Training starts from small random weights and bias and a learning coefficient α between 0 and 1. Widrow-Hoff learning assumes each unit corrects its weights using only locally available information: the input, the actual response and the intended response. The idea is to learn the error itself: where a Hebb matrix only reproduces associations exactly when few are stored, Widrow-Hoff keeps adding corrective terms proportional to the error (t − g) until the error is as small as possible. The loss is the mean squared error E = ½ Σ (t − y)²; the ½ only makes the derivative cleaner and does not change where the minimum is. The **gradient** of E with respect to the weights points in the direction of fastest increase of the error, so moving the weights in the opposite direction reduces it fastest (gradient descent). This gives the **delta rule**: wᵢ(new) = wᵢ(old) + α·(t − y_in)·xᵢ and b(new) = b(old) + α·(t − y_in), repeated until the largest weight change falls below a tolerance or a fixed number of epochs is reached. The **learning rate** matters: too large and the corrections grow without limit; too small and convergence is very slow; constant and the weights may keep oscillating around the best solution; decreasing over time and they converge, possibly before the error is fully corrected (a common choice is α = 1/n for n learning steps).

### GAIF-2.11 Limits of single-layer networks and the way out
Even with the delta rule, single-layer networks still misclassify some patterns. Demonstrating these limits was a major reason interest in neural networks declined in the 1970s. Interest revived in 1986 when Rumelhart and colleagues presented a way to train multi-layer networks: **backpropagation**, also called the generalised delta rule. Until the 1970s almost all models used linear functions, and a linear network can only represent linear relationships between inputs and outputs — stacking more linear layers does not add expressive power.

### GAIF-2.12 Families of neural networks
Hebb networks, perceptrons and Adaline are known as simple pattern-recognition networks. Other families include pattern-association networks (heteroassociative, autoassociative, bidirectional associative memory), competition-based networks (fixed-weight nets such as Maxnet, Mexican Hat and Hamming net; Kohonen self-organising maps; learning vector quantisation; counterpropagation), adaptive resonance theory (ART1, ART2), backpropagation networks, fixed-weight networks for constrained optimisation (continuous Hopfield net, Boltzmann, Cauchy and Gaussian machines), recurrent networks and backpropagation through time, and adaptive architectures (probabilistic neural nets, cascade correlation, deep learning, the neocognitron).

### GAIF-2.13 The generic (integrating) neuron
In 1972 Bruce Knight proposed the integrate-and-fire model: the neuron has an internal variable u(t), the membrane potential, that accumulates the incoming stimulus over time; when u reaches a trigger value θ, the neuron fires an impulse and u resets. Today's standard artificial neuron, the integrating neuron, works in two steps: first it sums its weighted inputs plus a bias, y_in = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + b, giving an activity level; then it passes that level through a **non-linear** activation function to produce its output. Inputs can be binary (0/1) or bipolar (−1/+1), and the weights are adjustable over the network's life, reflecting that some connections are stronger than others, as in biology.

### GAIF-2.14 Activation functions and vanishing gradients
The activation function determines a neuron's output from its inputs. The **sigmoid**, f(x) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ˣ), squashes any input into the range 0 to 1 and became essential for backpropagation and multilayer perceptrons; several other S-shaped functions behave similarly. But the sigmoid **saturates**: for very large positive or negative inputs its output flattens towards 1 or 0 and its derivative approaches 0. During backpropagation, gradients are multiplied layer by layer, so many small gradients multiply towards zero (0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 …) — the **vanishing gradient** problem, a major obstacle to training deep networks. The hyperbolic tangent (**tanh**) improved some properties but still saturates. The **ReLU** (rectified linear unit), f(z) = max(0, z), outputs 0 for negative inputs and the input itself for positive inputs. It works well because it does not saturate for positive inputs, is computationally very cheap (important for deep learning) and still introduces non-linearity; Glorot, Bordes and Bengio (2011) showed rectifier networks could be trained effectively and had advantages over sigmoid and tanh networks.

### GAIF-2.15 Why non-linear neurons matter
Non-linear neurons let a network learn complex, non-linear relationships between inputs and outputs: they can approximate curves, interactions and highly complex mappings that a straight line cannot represent; they create complex decision boundaries and solve non-linear classification problems such as XOR; and they enable deep learning, building hierarchical representations, learning interactions between features and improving complex pattern recognition.
