## DTGS-1 Digital transformation and change management

### DTGS-1.1 What digital transformation is
Several definitions are used: the integration of digital technology into all areas of a business, fundamentally changing how it operates and delivers value to customers (Red Hat, 2020); a continuous, complex undertaking that can substantially shape a company, whose strategies are cross-functional and must be aligned with other functional and operational strategies (Matt, Hess & Benlian, 2015); and a major organisational change induced by digital technologies (Barthel & Hess, 2019, and others). A recurring message is that digital transformation is not only about technology (Tabrizi et al., 2019). "Digital" means different things to different people — paperless, data analytics, AI, agile teams, teleconferencing — while "transformation" means change and alignment: transforming technology infrastructure, processes, the business model, products and services, work procedures and people, aligned with business strategy, in order to create value.

### DTGS-1.2 Building blocks of the DT process (Vial)
Vial (2021) describes digital transformation as a sequence: **disruptions** (changes in consumer behaviour and expectations, the competitive landscape, availability of data) fuel the **use of digital technologies** (social, mobile, analytics, internet of things, platforms and ecosystems) and trigger **strategic responses** (digital business strategy, digital transformation strategy). Digital technologies enable **changes in value-creation paths** (value propositions, value networks, digital channels, agility and ambidexterity). These are affected by **structural changes** (organisational structure, culture, leadership, employee roles and skills) and held back by **organisational barriers** (inertia, resistance). They generate **positive impacts** (operational efficiency, organisational performance, industry and society improvements) and **negative impacts** (security and privacy).

### DTGS-1.3 Five strategic domains (Rogers)
David Rogers (2016, *The Digital Transformation Playbook*) identifies five domains of strategy that digital technology transforms: **customers**, **competition**, **data**, **innovation** and **value**. The course uses this framework throughout, together with Rogers's readiness self-assessment, which evaluates a company's preparedness for digital transformation in two parts: strategic thinking and organisational agility.

### DTGS-1.4 Why people resist change
Kotter and Schlesinger ("Choosing Strategies for Change", 1979; see also Kotter, *Leading Change*, 1995) give four common reasons for resistance: **parochial self-interest** (people focus on what they personally will lose rather than on the organisation as a whole); **misunderstanding and lack of trust** (they do not understand the change and its implications and do not trust those proposing it); **different assessments** (they evaluate the situation differently from the initiators and see more costs than benefits); and **low tolerance for change** (fear of not being able to develop the new skills and behaviour required).

### DTGS-1.5 Six approaches to dealing with resistance
| Approach | When to use | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Education and communication | There is a lack of information or inaccurate information and analysis | Once persuaded, people often help implement the change | Very time-consuming if many people are involved |
| Participation and involvement | Initiators lack information to design the change and others have considerable power to resist | Participants become committed and their information is integrated into the plan | Time-consuming; participants may design an inappropriate change |
| Facilitation and support | People resist because of adjustment problems (fear they cannot adapt) | No other approach works as well for adjustment problems | Time-consuming, expensive, and can still fail |
| Negotiation and agreement | A group will clearly lose out and has considerable power to resist | A relatively easy way to avoid major resistance | Can become too expensive if it alerts others to negotiate for compliance |
| Manipulation and co-optation | Other tactics will not work or are too expensive | Quick and inexpensive | Future problems if people feel manipulated |
| Explicit and implicit coercion | Speed is essential and the initiators have considerable power | Speedy; can overcome any kind of resistance | Risky if it leaves people angry at the initiators |

### DTGS-1.6 Choosing a change strategy
Kotter and Schlesinger recommend: (1) analyse situational factors — the amount and kind of resistance anticipated, the initiators' power relative to the resisters, who has the data needed to design the change and the energy to implement it, and how urgent the change is; (2) determine the optimal speed of change; (3) choose the methods for managing resistance accordingly.

### DTGS-1.7 Transformation methodology and ADKAR
A holistic transformation methodology combines several disciplines orchestrated by "meta-management" (evaluating culture, values and communication, and understanding interdependencies): programme/project management, strategy management, value management (tangible and soft benefits, hidden costs, the business case), process management (review processes, find gaps, re-engineer), change management (define the need for change, involve everyone, lead the change), risk management, technology management (requirements, aligning technology with business strategy, a roadmap) and competence and training management. Prosci's **ADKAR** model describes the phases individuals go through in a change: **Awareness**, **Desire**, **Knowledge**, **Ability**, **Reinforcement**, supported by tools such as communication, sponsorship, roadmaps, coaching, training and resistance management.

### DTGS-1.8 Who should lead digital transformation?
Furr, Gaarlandt and Shipilov (2019) argue: do not put a digital expert in charge of digital transformation. Asked to choose between an insider with a proven track record but little digital knowledge, a young digital guru from a big tech company, or a consultant who advised on digital, their answer favours the insider — because transformation is about changing the organisation and its people, which requires credibility and knowledge of the business more than technical expertise.

### DTGS-1.9 Disruptive innovation: low-end and new-market
Recent drivers of change include the COVID-19 pandemic, crypto and generative AI. Two types of disruptive innovation are distinguished. **Low-end innovations** offer characteristics similar to existing technologies at a substantially lower cost and "good enough" quality (e.g. Netflix against Blockbuster; open model hubs such as Hugging Face making AI accessible). **New-market disruptions** create a market that did not exist, such as the explosive growth of generative AI software for images, video, design, code, text, voice and music. See DTGS-3.18 for Christensen's full argument.

### DTGS-1.10 Generative AI in organisations
GenAI is used through the web (e.g. ChatGPT), on desktops (including open-source models that run locally, such as GPT4All), in mobile apps, integrated into existing software (e.g. Copilot) and in devices (wearables, smart glasses, AI pins, voice assistants). In a Gartner (2024) survey, organisations adopted GenAI mainly for customer experience (38%), revenue growth (26%), cost optimisation (17%) and business continuity (7%), with 12% citing none of these. Business capabilities of GenAI include content creation, summarisation, classification, chatbot performance improvement, planning and scheduling, optimisation, personalisation, extraction and reformatting, code generation, search and synthesis (Nsight, 2024).

### DTGS-1.11 Leadership and the future of work
GenAI is transforming the way we work: processes must be reshaped and redesigned, organisations need to change and embrace it, employees need upskilling and reskilling, and there are ethical challenges. The "co-intelligence" effect describes the synergy between humans and AI in decision-making, creativity and problem-solving — human-machine collaboration rather than replacement. Research also studies how the perceived "coolness" of GenAI tools fosters continuous engagement with them (Barreiras et al., 2026).

## DTGS-2 Innovation and digital business strategy

### DTGS-2.1 Invention versus innovation
**Innovation** is the process of bringing an invention, creation or insight about a novel device, process or system to its first successful commercial use; it applies to new goods, processes and services, and also to new markets, supply sources and organisational structures. Freeman (1982) distinguishes **invention** — an event, when a new idea for a product, process or service is thought up for the first time — from **innovation** — the process that moves from the original invention to its first successful commercialisation.

### DTGS-2.2 Divergent and convergent experiments
Rogers (2016) argues that successful innovation needs both exploratory learning (to generate and develop ideas) and confirmatory learning (to verify and refine them), through two kinds of experiment. **Divergent experiments** create choices: informal design (e.g. putting a prototype in customers' hands), an open set of questions, the right customers rather than average ones, variable sample size, focus on overall impressions and meaning, a prototype as rough as possible, used for idea generation in early stages. **Convergent experiments** make choices: formal scientific design (e.g. an A/B feature test or a pricing test), a precise question, a representative sample with test and control groups and statistical validity, focus on direct causality, used for optimisation in late stages. Both increase knowledge, test assumptions, look outside for answers and require a willingness to learn rather than just decide. They map onto two modes of thinking: **divergent** (creative, lateral, imagination, many ideas from one question) and **convergent** (critical, analytical, logic, many facts towards one answer). For the GenAI-era correction, see DTGS-3.13.

### DTGS-2.3 Open and collaborative innovation
**Open innovation** (Chesbrough, 2006) is the purposive use of knowledge inflows and outflows to accelerate internal innovation and to expand the markets for external use of innovation. **Collaborative innovation** is how people with a collective vision interact, offer ideas and share knowledge and experience for a common goal — a value co-creation process that produces a desired outcome for all collaborators while raising each one's capabilities.

### DTGS-2.4 Three balances of digital business strategy
Brynjolfsson and McAfee (*Machine, Platform, Crowd*, 2017) describe digital business strategy as balancing three pairs: **mind and machine** (which judgements to hand to machines and which to keep), **product and platform** (selling an output or hosting the exchange in which outputs are produced) and **core and crowd** (organising around internal expertise or around a distributed crowd of talent). How GenAI shifts each balance is covered in DTGS-3.4.

### DTGS-2.5 Three routes out of a shrinking or stable market
Rogers (2016) plots value proposition (same or new) against customers or use case (same or new). From the current position a business can move to **new value** for the same customers, to **new customers** with the same value, or to **both**. In the AI-era reading of Class 3, the three routes are: **take the last profits** (manage the decline deliberately — harvest margin, cut cost, exit on your own timetable); **move to an adjacent market** (apply the assets that keep their value where they remain scarce); and **redefine what you sell** (change the unit of value so the disruptive technology becomes an input to your offering rather than a substitute). Applied to the 99designs case (a design-contest platform facing AI image generation): keep contests for buyers who need human accountability; sell curation and validation of AI-generated options; or sell the validated decision or an attestation of human authorship, with the artwork becoming a cost line.

### DTGS-2.6 The value proposition roadmap
Rogers's (2016) value proposition roadmap has six steps: (1) identify the key customer types by the value they receive; (2) define the current value for each customer (value elements and overall proposition); (3) identify emerging threats (new technology, changing needs, competitors and substitutes); (4) assess the strength of the current value elements; (5) generate new potential value elements (from new technology, sociocultural and business trends, unmet needs); (6) synthesise a new forward-looking value proposition. Its application to AI is in DTGS-3.12.

### DTGS-2.7 The Business Model Canvas
Osterwalder and Pigneur's Business Model Canvas describes how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value in nine blocks. The **frontstage**: (1) **customer segments** — who we target (mass market, niche, segmented); (2) **value proposition** — what value we create by satisfying needs or solving problems; (3) **channels** — how we reach customers (communication, distribution, sales); (4) **customer relationships** — what type of relationship we have (personal assistance, self-service, automated, co-creation) and how we attract and retain customers; (9) **revenue streams** — how we make money (one-time sale, subscription, advertising). The **backstage**: (5) **key activities** we must master; (6) **key resources** (physical, financial, human, intellectual such as patents); (7) **key partners** (suppliers, alliances, cooperation); and (8) **cost structure** (the most important costs; cost-driven versus value-driven). Value flows upstream (from partners), within the firm, and downstream (to customers). Business models must evolve: Nespresso's canvas is used to ask whether a model is still current, Tesla's to redraw it with each iteration (Supercharger network, Gigafactory, add-ons) and Amazon's to recall n-sided platforms. The Value Proposition Canvas zooms into blocks 1 and 2: customer jobs, pains and gains on one side; products and services, pain relievers and gain creators on the other.

## DTGS-3 Applied AI for business models

### DTGS-3.1 The value gap: everyone has adopted, few have captured
In McKinsey's 2026 global survey (1,719 respondents in 97 countries), 88% of organisations use AI in at least one business function and 80% of people report individual productivity gains, but only 37% attribute any EBIT impact to AI, and just 6% are "high performers" (at least 5% of EBIT from AI). Adoption is now table stakes, not a competitive position; the share reporting EBIT impact has been flat for two years while investment rose; and half the high performers aim to transform the business rather than merely improve it, mostly by redesigning workflows. MIT Project NANDA's "GenAI Divide" (2025) reported that 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact against an estimated US$30–40 billion invested, diagnosing a "learning gap": systems could not retain feedback, adapt to the context of the work or fit existing processes — the tool sat beside the workflow, not inside it. Critics note its narrow success test (measurable ROI within about six months), thin, non-peer-reviewed evidence base and exclusion of classical machine learning; the criticism attacks the precision of the number, not its direction.

### DTGS-3.2 The agentic hype and why value leaks
Gartner places agentic AI at the peak of inflated expectations: 17% of organisations have deployed AI agents, over 60% expect to within two years, and over 40% of agentic AI projects are predicted to be cancelled by the end of 2027, for three reasons — escalating costs (agent runs consume tokens non-linearly with autonomy, retries and tool calls), unclear business value (most "agentic" use cases do not need agents) and inadequate risk controls. Model capability is not on the list, and "agent washing" (relabelling products as agents) is common: only about 130 of the thousands of vendors claiming agents are considered real. Value leaks out in four places: **integration** (the pilot lives beside the process, so nothing compounds — a fluent assistant that cannot act is a demonstration, not a capability); **fragmented data** (customer reality is scattered across systems, so the model sees a partial picture and generalises confidently); **weak governance** (no evaluation set, escalation path or owner; probabilistic outputs pushed through routines designed for deterministic ones); and **misaligned incentives** (channel owners optimise engagement while operations absorb broken AI promises). GenAI reshapes value creation, delivery and capture at the same time, so a change in one business-model block puts pressure on several others (Li, Qu & Divekar, 2026).

### DTGS-3.3 We have seen this before: DT failures and the change iceberg
AI programmes in 2026 fail the way digital transformation programmes failed a decade earlier. Kotter (1995) listed: no sense of urgency, no powerful guiding team, no vision or a vision not communicated, insufficient planning and consolidation, new approaches never institutionalised. AI programmes fail through unclear business value, tools beside the workflow, no owner or evaluation set or escalation path, costs escalating before workflows are redesigned, and pilots never institutionalised. The **change iceberg** shows why: above the waterline sit technology, tools, licences, architecture and the demo; below sit incentives, ownership, skills, trust, status and fear of redundancy. Ward and Uhl (2012) found about 30% of transformation projects fail outright and only about 30% fully succeed — similar to the share now reporting AI EBIT impact — suggesting the binding constraint was never the technology.

### DTGS-3.4 Triage: feature or business model?
A business model is "the architecture of the firm's mechanisms for creating, delivering and capturing value" (Teece, 2010). Ask three questions of every AI proposal, in order, before discussing technology: does it change how we **create** value (new or better outputs, faster cycles — easiest, and where most pilots stop, which is why they show no P&L effect)? How we **deliver** value (new channels, relationships or segments — harder, needs integration and brand discipline; where customer trust is won or lost)? How we **capture** value (new pricing units, tiers, cost structure, defensible position — hardest and least attempted; if nothing changes here, you have bought a cost line, not a strategy)? AI that touches only creation is a feature. The organising claim: adoption is not a strategy — a business model is. Revisiting the three balances (DTGS-2.4) for GenAI: **mind and machine** — the line moves further into language and analysis than ever, and becomes a weekly management decision; **product and platform** — GenAI restores a real marginal cost per interaction, changing platform economics for the first time in twenty years; **core and crowd** — the model is a third option, a synthetic intermediary that has absorbed the crowd's output.

### DTGS-3.5 Moravec's paradox, 2026 edition
Hans Moravec (1988) observed that it is comparatively easy to make computers perform like adults on intelligence tests and hard to give them the perception and mobility of a one-year-old. The paradox is really about which capabilities have abundant, cheaply encodable training signal: language had it; judgement under accountability does not. In 2026, machines are clearly better at drafting, summarising and reformatting at volume, translating between formats and languages, recalling patterns across large corpora and producing many plausible options; humans are clearly better at deciding what the customer actually wants, absorbing accountability for a wrong answer, reading a room and choosing the option that will survive contact with reality; contested ground includes first-pass triage of ambiguous requests, diagnosis from sparse evidence, multi-step execution across live systems and judging whether an output is good enough. Strategically, the human column is your pricing power and the contested column your risk register.

### DTGS-3.6 The AI value stack
Seven layers, commodity at the bottom and differentiation at the top: (1) **compute and infrastructure** — capital-intensive, consolidating, commodity; (2) **foundation models** — prices falling fast, capability converging, commodity; (3) **orchestration and tooling** (prompting, retrieval, agent frameworks, evaluation harnesses) — rented and replaceable; (4) **context and data layer** (your documents, records, exceptions, tacit rules) — scarce, cumulative, hard to copy, **yours**; (5) **workflow and accountability** (where output enters the process, who verifies it, what escalates) — **yours**; (6) **interface and experience** — visible and imitable within a quarter, rented; (7) **governance and assurance** (evaluation, audit trail, disclosure, redress) — becoming a licence to operate. Anything you can buy with a credit card is not a moat; the moat is the context you accumulate and the accountability you can prove. Only unpublished context counts: a tool that can extract a brand's tone, fonts and style from its public website in seconds (e.g. Google's Pomelli experiment) shows public context is not proprietary.

### DTGS-3.7 Make or buy: five rungs
Choosing how to put a model into a process is choosing a cost, control and risk profile:
1. **Off-the-shelf tool** (licensed assistant or copilot) — low per-seat cost, low control, risk of shadow AI and data leakage, days to value; use when the task is generic and data is not sensitive.
2. **Prompt and template layer** (standard instructions, house style, reusable outputs) — very low cost, medium control, 1–3 weeks; use when quality varies more between people than between models.
3. **Retrieval (RAG)** grounded in your own documents — medium cost, high control, retrieval quality becomes the ceiling, 1–3 months; use when answers must be traceable to your own sources.
4. **Fine-tuning or adapters** — medium-high recurring cost, risk of deprecation when the base model moves, 2–4 months; use when format and behaviour matter more than knowledge.
5. **Agents and orchestration** across systems — high, non-linear cost, highest control only if instrumented, risk of autonomy without accountability, 3–9 months; use when work is multi-step, high-volume and verifiable.

Most organisations skip rungs 2 and 3 and jump to 5, then discover the ceiling was the context they never assembled. **Decision rule:** choose the lowest rung that meets the requirement and climb one rung only when you can name, in one sentence, the constraint the current rung cannot meet and who will verify the next one does (e.g. 2→3: "we cannot answer without citing our own current documents"). If you cannot define what a correct output looks like, stop — the cheapest decision of all.

### DTGS-3.8 Make or buy inverted, and the non-negotiables
Coding agents lower the cost of building, so buying software becomes a build decision: McKinsey reports 32% of organisations decided not to buy a software product because agentic coding tools let them build it in-house. This gives buyers new negotiating leverage but new exposure: building is cheap, owning is not. Three questions decide: is it differentiating (customer-visible and specific → build; plumbing → buy)? Can we maintain it for five years (evaluation sets, model migrations, security, staff turnover)? Who carries the liability (a vendor contract transfers some risk; your own build transfers none)? Rule of thumb: build the context and the workflow; buy the model, infrastructure and plumbing. Three assets must never be outsourced: the **context layer** (with full export rights in a documented format), the **evaluation set** (100–300 real cases graded by your own experts, refreshed quarterly — needed to compare vendors, detect regressions and prove quality to a regulator) and the **audit trail** (what was asked, answered, changed by a human and why — log the human correction, the most valuable part). Brynjolfsson and McAfee's platform questions apply to model providers: does building on this model commoditise what we sell; are we competing on judgement or price; whose brand faces the customer when it fails? Whoever controls the interface, outcome data and the toolkit for trust and matching captures the value.

### DTGS-3.9 Unit economics: the price paradox and cost per verified outcome
The cost of querying a GPT-3.5-level model fell from about $20 to $0.07 per million tokens between late 2022 and late 2024 — roughly 280-fold (Stanford AI Index 2025) — and the decline is steepening. Yet few make money, because the token is not the main cost: retrieval infrastructure, human verification, rework, escalation, evaluation when models update and governance do not deflate; they are wage- and process-linked, so as the model becomes nearly free, human judgement becomes almost all of the cost. A worked customer-service example (100,000 cases a month) gives a total **cost per verified outcome** of about €1.36, of which model inference is only €0.02 (1.5%); escalation (€0.70) and rework (€0.33) dominate, so raising containment from 82% to 90% would cut unit cost by about a third, and removing verification saves €0.21 but opens an unbounded rework tail. The dashboard metric is: cost per verified outcome = (model + retrieval + verification + escalation + rework + governance cost) ÷ outcomes accepted by a human or a defined automatic test, tracked with four numbers — **volume**, **containment** (share resolved without a human, the single most valuable operational number), **verification rate** (should fall but never reach zero) and **defect escape rate** (wrong and shipped). An outcome nobody checked is an assumption: vendors count deflections, customers count resolutions. Governance rule: no AI initiative gets a second budget round without a cost per verified outcome for the first.

### DTGS-3.10 Hidden liabilities and the shape of the promise
Costs that never reach the business case: **workslop** (AI-generated work that looks finished but lacks substance — in a BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab survey of 1,150 US desk workers (2025), 40% had received some in the last month, losing nearly two hours per incident, 53% admitted sending it themselves, and about half rated the sender as less capable and reliable); **knowledge decay** (low-quality outputs compound across teams and degrade the organisation's knowledge base); **model deprecation** (every fine-tune and prompt library must be re-tested when the base model changes); and **trust cost**. Verification work is displaced downstream: the sender saves twenty minutes and colleagues spend hours, so a business case showing only the producer's time saved measures one side of the ledger. Costs are front-loaded and savings back-loaded: data preparation and integration, then parallel running with verification overhead, then workflow redesign as containment rises, and only then savings exceeding cost. To shorten the dip: pick one workflow, instrument containment from day one, and refuse to scale until the cost per verified outcome has fallen for two quarters. Every AI business case has two charts — the optimistic one for the investment committee and the realistic one for operations; the professional skill is to show the realistic one first and still get the budget.

### DTGS-3.11 Stress-testing the business model with GenAI
The canvas is an instrument, not a poster. Three rules: never fill a block in isolation (a customer-service assistant is simultaneously a channel, relationship, activity, resource and cost decision); write the **friction** next to every **promise**; and end each block with a **question** for the executive committee, not a plan. Simply overlaying GenAI on the nine blocks underestimates their interdependencies and the organisational friction of changing them. Highlights per block: **customer segments** (moderate pressure) — behavioural, real-time segmentation, but fragmented data and noise; **value propositions** (high) — hyper-personalised and AI-native offers, but hallucination, tone drift and the gap between an impressive prototype and a reliable service; **channels** (high) — interfaces become dialogue, but a channel that can talk but not act creates work ("can this channel act, or only talk?"); **customer relationships** (high) — always-on, proactive and personal, or transactional and deflecting, and trust collapses after one insensitive answer in sensitive contexts; **key resources** (high) — domain data, AI-capable people, model access and governance know-how, often overestimated; **key activities** (high) — drafting and analysis compress while review, exceptions and escalation become the real work ("have we redesigned the workflow, or accelerated the old one?"); **key partners** (moderate) — cloud, model and implementation dependencies become strategic choices about control and risk; **revenue streams** (high) — AI-native products and usage-based tiers, but GenAI is often bundled without separate pricing so margin falls as usage grows; **cost structure** (high) — front-loaded, usage-based and volatile, with monitoring, red-teaming and evaluation rarely budgeted.

### DTGS-3.12 The value proposition roadmap applied to AI
Applying Rogers's value proposition roadmap (DTGS-2.6) to AI stops a feature from being mistaken for a value proposition. Class 3 reads it as six questions: (1) which customers — the segment that feels the pain, not the easiest to reach; (2) what value they get today, in the customer's words; (3) what is changing for them, including that they now have a model of their own; (4) what emerging value we could deliver — the candidate list; (5) which of those is most valuable to them — ask them, which is where nine of ten options die; (6) can we deliver it reliably — an industrialisation test of reliability, tone, escalation and a verification gate. GenAI **helps** at the candidate-list step, generating a far wider, cheap and genuinely divergent set of options, but **hurts** at the choosing step, where it will confidently rank the options without ever having met a customer: that step belongs to the customer.

### DTGS-3.13 Divergent and convergent experiments after GenAI
Rogers assumed divergent and convergent experiments (DTGS-2.2) cost about the same, so balancing them was a matter of sequencing. GenAI made divergence (generating options) roughly a hundred times cheaper and left convergence (customer time and expert judgement) unchanged, so attention, not generation, is now the constraint, and the risk is choosing on fluency. Balance is now a budget decision: for every hour spent generating options, book an hour of customer or expert time to eliminate them, and let no option enter convergent testing without a stated falsification criterion.

### DTGS-3.14 Where business-model innovation stalls, and the matrix of change
Multiple-case evidence (eight firms, 2024) shows firms innovate **value creation** relatively easily, **value propositions** with difficulty and **value capture** hardest of all, because capture means changing pricing, tiers, contracts and cost structure and confronting cannibalisation of existing revenue. Incumbents find architectural business-model innovation hardest, so attention is most valuable in the block everyone skips. The **Matrix of Change** (Brynjolfsson, Renshaw & Van Alstyne, 1997) lists current and target practices and marks each pair as reinforcing (+), interfering (−) or independent. The canvas shows the destination; the matrix shows whether you can get there and in what order, because two sensible changes can destroy value during the transition (e.g. "deflect more contacts with an agent" and "raise first-contact resolution" interfere, since deflection removes the easy cases). Do reinforcing pairs first and never start two interfering changes in the same quarter.

### DTGS-3.15 Value capture: servitization and outcome pricing
Outcome pricing is not new: **servitization** and product-service systems moved from buying a product (the buyer carries the risk of failure) to product plus service, to buying the outcome — e.g. jet engine "power by the hour", where reliability becomes the supplier's interest. AI-native product-service systems sell resolved cases, booked meetings or verified decisions; the marginal cost is inference rather than materials, and the unit is disputable. The model spreads now because measuring outcomes became cheap. Software pricing is moving from per seat, to per use, to per outcome. Published 2026 per-resolution rates in customer service differ about fourfold for nominally the same unit (roughly $0.50 at HubSpot, $0.99 at Fin, $1.50 at Zendesk and $2.00 at Salesforce Agentforce); HubSpot moved from $1.00 per conversation to $0.50 per resolved conversation in April 2026; Sierra built its whole business on pure outcome pricing; 11x charges per booked meeting rather than per seat. When the AI does the work, the seat stops being the unit of consumption. **The definition of the billable unit is the whole bill**: one vendor's "resolution" may include customers who simply left, another bills only verified resolutions. A better rate on a worse definition is a worse deal — negotiate the definition before the price, asking to see a conversation that bills and one that does not, who decides it was resolved, what happens to the bill when the model improves, and whether seat costs remain.

### DTGS-3.16 The economics of free, inverted
Anderson (2009) described four ways to make something free in order to sell something else, and GenAI changes each: **direct cross-subsidy** still works but the free tier now has a real marginal cost, so it needs a designed ceiling; **three-party markets** (advertising) are under pressure because if the model answers, the user never reaches the page the advertiser paid for; **freemium** is inverted, because the most enthusiastic free users become the largest variable cost; and **non-monetary markets** (contributions for reputation or recognition) are destabilised when a model absorbs contributions without giving recognition. For the first time in twenty years, a digital business has a real marginal cost per interaction.

### DTGS-3.17 Six ways to compete on business models
Classic levers, re-tested for GenAI: **switching costs** (iPod/iTunes) — strengthened, since accumulated context and evaluation sets become the new lock-in if you own them; **recurring revenue** (Nespresso capsules) — strengthened, since per-outcome pricing gives recurrence without a physical consumable; **revenue before you sell** (Dell build-to-order) — neutral; **game-changing cost structure** (VoIP versus telephony) — weakened, as a cost advantage can now be bought by a competitor; **getting others to do the work** (Facebook users) — at risk, since the model can replace the crowd and remove its reason to contribute; **scalability** (serving one more customer for nothing) — weakened, because inference gives each interaction a real cost. Two of six levers rest on near-zero marginal cost, so strategies that relied on them need rewriting.

### DTGS-3.18 Cannibalisation and Christensen's disruptive innovation
Disruptive innovation (Christensen, 1997) is not a breakthrough technology but a simpler, cheaper product whose performance falls below what established customers value, aimed at a market too small to matter — which is why well-managed incumbents rationally ignore it while serving their best customers better. AI customer service followed the pattern: worse at first, allowed in on the cheap tier; good enough on simple cases, which are most of the volume; now climbing into contested work. Incumbents face the dilemma publicly: a native competitor can adopt pure outcome pricing with no seat revenue to protect, while an incumbent runs several pricing models at once to avoid cannibalising itself, and may end up acquiring the native: in June 2026 Salesforce signed a definitive agreement to acquire Fin (formerly Intercom), the leading per-resolution player, for about $3.6 billion — when you cannot reprice yourself, you acquire the price. The barrier to repricing is rarely analytical; it is the revenue and incentives you would cannibalise. If you cannot reprice yourself, you can acquire, partner, or be repriced by someone else.

### DTGS-3.19 The EU AI Act as a market maker
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024 with staggered application. As presented in the lecture (status September 2026, after the "Digital Omnibus" amendments of June 2026): Article 50 transparency obligations — telling people they are dealing with AI and marking synthetic content — apply from 2 August 2026; legacy systems and new prohibitions follow on 2 December 2026; high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems (hiring, credit scoring, education, essential services) are deferred to 2 December 2027, and for AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I) to 2 August 2028. Fines reach €35 million or 7% of turnover for prohibited practices and €15 million or 3% for transparency and high-risk breaches. Regulation is not only a cost: if you put a GenAI agent in front of EU customers, disclosure is already a product requirement, and the ability to prove what your AI did and guarantee a human alternative becomes something you can sell — compliance as a value proposition.

### DTGS-3.20 Klarna and "AI raises the floor"
Klarna's customer-service deployment is the most instructive public case because it was reversed in public. In February 2024 it launched an AI assistant handling 2.3 million conversations in its first month, equated to about 700 full-time agents, cutting resolution time from 11 to under 2 minutes (company-reported claims). In May 2025 its CEO said the focus on cost had led to lower quality; humans were reintroduced for disputes, fraud and hardship, with a commitment that customers can always reach a human, because satisfaction fell on complex and emotional cases even when answers were correct. By 2026 the framing settled: AI handles the simple tier and human service becomes almost a "VIP" premium tier — a business-model decision, not a technology one. Causal evidence from a staggered rollout to 5,172 support agents (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025) found a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour, but +30–34% for the least experienced (agents with two months' tenure performed like untreated agents with over six months) and a slight quality drop for the most skilled. AI raises the floor but not the ceiling: variance compresses, the routine cases through which juniors learned disappear (raising the question of where future experts come from), and service quality differentiates only at the top — an argument for a premium human tier.

### DTGS-3.21 The downside register
- **Liability** — In *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (2024), an airline chatbot wrongly told a passenger he could claim a bereavement fare retroactively within 90 days, while another page of the same site said the opposite; the tribunal found negligent misrepresentation, rejected the defence that the chatbot was a separate legal entity and held the company responsible for all information on its website (about C$650 in damages). The award was small, but the precedent is global: deflection transfers work, never liability, and disclosing that it was AI discharges a transparency duty, not a duty of care.
- **Personalised pricing** — the US FTC (2025) found pricing intermediaries using location, browsing and purchase history, customer-service interactions, demographics and even mouse movements to set individual prices; in 2026 it moved to require disclosure. Pricing the same good differently by inferred personal traits engages consumer law, GDPR and the AI Act in the EU. A peer-reviewed study of UK Uber drivers (Binns et al., 2025) found commission rose from about 25% to about 29% after dynamic pricing arrived in 2023, sometimes over half the fare, with real hourly pay falling from over £22 to just over £19; Uber denies personalising rider prices, and the attributes used are undisclosed.
- **Model collapse** — training indiscriminately on model-generated content causes irreversible defects in which the tails of the original distribution disappear (Shumailov et al., *Nature*, 2024). Commercially, the tail is the rare case your best people are paid to recognise; AI-drafted notes entering CRMs and knowledge bases contaminate tomorrow's retrieval sources.
- **Automation bias** — people supervising automated systems accept recommendations without adequate checking and miss errors they would have caught themselves, more so as the system becomes more reliable (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). If nobody measures the override rate, or it is under about 2%, the human in the loop is decorative; showing two candidates instead of one confident answer restores judgement.

Each danger needs a measurable early-warning sign, a control and an owner, e.g.: a written cap on what an agent may promise (legal and channel owner); attribute review and outcome testing before personalised pricing (pricing and data protection); provenance marking of model-written records (data owner); publishing the override rate monthly (process owner); a protected apprenticeship path so juniors still handle routine cases (HR); and a contractual, annually tested context-export right (procurement). Principles cannot be audited; a register with thresholds and owners can.

### DTGS-3.22 Invention is not innovation: from prototype to portfolio
A working prototype can now be built live in about twenty minutes. The class built one for **Ao Vivo**, a fictional "human premium layer": an AI-native business whose product is a guaranteed, attested human interaction that any company with an AI front line can plug in and charge for, with the mandatory Article 50 disclosure as its point of sale. As an online-to-offline platform its dashboard needs two numbers — cost per verified outcome (is the business viable?) and a liquidity metric, time to attested human (is the promise real?). Yet the prototype is an **invention**, not an **innovation** (DTGS-2.1): innovation needs months, contracts, a regulator and a first paying client. GenAI collapsed the cost of invention and left the cost of innovation almost untouched, so organisations risk dozens of impressive artefacts and no commercialisations. The invention test is "can we show it?"; the innovation test is "has someone outside paid for it, twice?". The take-home question: if a working prototype takes twenty minutes, what exactly is your moat? (Not technology, speed of adoption or a model partnership.) The **Applied AI for business models worksheet** gives one row per use case with eight columns: use case (one sentence in the language of the work), blocks touched (at least three), mechanism (creation, delivery or capture), implementation rung with its named constraint, unit of value (seat, use or outcome), verification gate (who checks, what share, what escalates), risk and obligation (e.g. Article 50, high-risk, whose logo carries the failure) and the one metric (cost per verified outcome and today's baseline). For **portfolio prioritisation**, score value (V), feasibility (F) and risk (R) from 1 to 5 and multiply: V × F × (6 − R). Multiply, never average, so a spectacular value cannot hide an unacceptable risk; any single 1 is a veto for this cycle; score value at realistic, not demo, containment; feasibility is 1 if no named person can judge whether an output is correct; re-score quarterly. Example: an insurance first-response drafter scores 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, an autonomous hardship-decision agent 5 × 2 × 1 = 10.

### DTGS-3.23 The honest AI maturity model
A tongue-in-cheek five-level model: (1) **denial** ("our business is different, our data is not ready"), ending when a competitor issues a press release; (2) **enthusiasm** — everyone has a licence, nobody has a baseline; (3) **pilot purgatory** — many proofs of concept, no changed processes, where most organisations are; (4) **grief** — the first usage-based invoice and the first complaint that was "technically answered correctly"; (5) **adulthood** — one workflow, one metric, one named owner and a stopping rule: boring, documented and profitable. Trying to skip levels 3 and 4 is level 3 with better slides. Adoption is finished; capture is the job.
